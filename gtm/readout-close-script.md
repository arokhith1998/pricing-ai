# Readout-to-pilot close script

The readout call is the only call in the funnel that converts revenue. This is the script.
The diagnostic was free; this call is where the free work earns the right to name the offer:
a $15K, 90-day pilot, fee due at signature, 50% credited toward an annual subscription
signed within the pilot window.

The pilot's success criterion is USAGE, not deliverables: at least 20 deals run through
guidance (approvals and exception requests logged) inside the 90 days. Say this on the
call. It tells the champion what good looks like, it is the evidence an investor will ask
us for, and a pilot that only consumed readouts is a failed pilot even if it renews.

One rule above all: their numbers carry the call. You are not presenting Pricekeel. You are
presenting their book, with receipts, and the offer is one sentence near the end.

## Pre-call checklist (no call until every box is checked)

- [ ] **Advisor sign-off obtained** on this specific readout. No real-data readout goes out
      the door without it. Non-negotiable gate.
- [ ] **Segment-aware reference fix verified** in this readout. The global-reference version
      overstates Enterprise excess and a sharp Head of Pricing will catch it. Confirm the
      headline number was produced with per-segment references, not the global 5%.
- [ ] **Headline number triple-checked**: re-run the diagnostic from the raw file, confirm
      the LoadReport disclosures (rows read, dupes dropped, open excluded, blank amounts
      excluded) match what you are about to say, and confirm the number in the deck equals
      the number in the engine output. If we are wrong about our own headline, the product
      thesis dies on this call.
- [ ] **CFO invited, by the champion, not by us.** Ask the champion a week out: "Your CFO
      will want to see this. Want to forward the invite, or should the readout be
      CFO-forwardable and you walk it in yourself?" If no CFO, confirm the champion can
      sponsor a $15K signature alone.
- [ ] **Top-5 deals rehearsed.** For each: deal, discount given, reference for that segment,
      the gap in dollars, the flag that drove it (rep, quarter-end, missing approval). Know
      them cold, no reading off the slide.
- [ ] **"What we would not change" section present.** The sales-defendable list. Without it
      the readout reads as a witch hunt and the CRO kills the pilot later.
- [ ] **Caveats rehearsed out loud.** Correlational, not causal. Upper bound, not a refund.
      Say them before they ask; it is the trust move that separates us from a slide deck.
- [ ] **Calendar held for the next step** before the call starts: a specific date you can
      offer for pilot kickoff, and your own WIP check done (cap is 2 concurrent diagnostics;
      do not offer a kickoff date you cannot staff).

## Call agenda (45 minutes)

| Time | Segment |
|---|---|
| 0-3 min | Lead with their headline number |
| 3-18 min | Walk the top-5 deals with receipts |
| 18-25 min | The trap-check question |
| 25-32 min | The offer, named in one sentence |
| 32-40 min | Next step with a date |
| 40-45 min | Buffer for the CFO's questions |

### 1. Lead with their headline number (minute 0)

No agenda slide, no company background, no "a bit about me." First sentence:

> "Last year, [Company] booked [X]% of list price. The gap between that and your reference
> discounts is [$Y] in excess discounting, concentrated in [segment / quarter-end / N reps].
> Everything I am about to show you is the receipts behind that one sentence, and I will
> also tell you exactly where that number is an upper bound and why."

Then pause. Let the number sit. The CFO's first question writes the rest of the call.

Immediately after the headline, the disclosure block, unprompted:

> "Before the findings: what we read from your file. [N] rows received, [N] duplicates
> dropped, [N] open deals excluded, [N] rows with blank amounts excluded from the band
> analysis and disclosed, not silently zeroed. If any of those counts surprise you, we stop
> here and reconcile first."

### 2. Walk the top-5 deals with receipts (minutes 3-18)

Five deals, one screen each: deal ID, account, segment, discount given, the reference for
that segment, the dollar gap, and the flag (rep, quarter-end timing, no approval on record).

Phrasing per deal:

> "[OPP-ID], [segment], closed at [X]% against a [Y]% reference for that segment. The flag
> here is [quarter-end close / REP-NN, who appears in [M] of these five / no approver
> recorded]. The receipt: [the comparable deals that set the reference]. If your team has
> context I cannot see in the CSV, say so; that context is exactly what the pilot adds."

After deal five, the counterweight, always:

> "And here is what I would not change. [Segment / rep behavior] is working: [one sentence].
> This is not a case for discounting less everywhere. It is a case for knowing which
> discounts buy wins and which give margin away."

### 3. The trap-check question (minutes 18-25)

This is the pivot. Ask it, then stop talking:

> "If you had caught these five before they closed, not thirty days after, what would that
> have been worth?"

Let them answer. Their answer is the pilot's business case, in their words, in front of
their CFO. Do not supply a number for them. If they lowball, ask:

> "Is that just these five, or does that rate hold across the [N] deals in the same flag
> categories?"

If they ask "well, what do YOU think it is worth," redirect honestly:

> "The diagnostic is retrospective and correlational, so I will not promise a recovery
> number. What I can do is put the catch-it-before-close loop in place and let your next
> quarter's deals answer the question with your data. That is what the pilot is."

### 4. The offer, named in one sentence (minutes 25-32)

The transition from findings to offer, verbatim:

> "Everything you have seen today is a rear-view mirror. The reason it exists is to show
> you the mirror works. The product is pointing it forward."

Then the one sentence:

> "The pilot is ninety days, fifteen thousand dollars, due at signature: we stand up this
> readout as a monthly cadence on your live closed deals, with the decision log, and if you
> sign an annual subscription within the ninety days, half the pilot fee is credited to it."

Follow with the three clarifiers, briefly, before they ask:

- "The annual subscription is thirty to sixty thousand a year, scaled on your deal volume.
  You will know your exact number before the pilot ends; nothing is sprung on you."
- "The credit applies only if the annual is signed inside the pilot window, and it claws
  back pro-rata if the annual is terminated early. I would rather say that now than have
  your counsel find it."
- "Each readout lands five business days after I confirm your intake file is valid. Business
  days after validated intake, not a calendar date I might miss, not a date that slips
  because your export needed a second pass."

### 5. Next step with a date (minutes 32-40)

Never end on "think it over." End on a date:

> "If this is a yes, here is the path: I send the pilot agreement today, you sign and pay,
> and your first monthly cycle starts [specific date]. The first live readout lands five
> business days after your first validated intake. Does [date] work as the start?"

If yes: confirm the date, confirm the intake contact, confirm who joins the monthly
decision-review call. Done.

If not yet, see the stalls below.

## The three most likely stalls, and the replies

### "I need to socialize this internally."

> "That is the right move, and the readout is built for it: it is forwardable as a PDF and
> it leads with the disclosure block, so finance can audit it rather than take my word.
> Two asks. First, who specifically needs to see it, and can I tailor one page to their
> question? Second, can we put a date on the follow-up now: same time, two weeks out? If
> the answer at that point is no, I will take the no."

Why this works: it helps the socializing instead of fighting it, and it converts an open
loop into a dated one. Never leave the call without the follow-up date.

### "Send me a proposal."

> "There is no forty-page proposal; what I just said is the whole commercial structure, and
> I will put it in a one-page agreement today: ninety days, fifteen thousand at signature,
> monthly readout five business days after validated intake, fifty percent credited to an
> annual signed inside the window, no auto-renewal. What it cannot include yet is your
> start date, because I cap concurrent engagements at two and slots assign at signature.
> Which question does the proposal need to answer that I have not answered today?"

Why this works: "send a proposal" is usually either a polite no or a hidden question. The
last line surfaces which one it is. The capacity statement is true (WIP cap of 2) and gives
the date real scarcity without manufacturing any.

### "What happens to our data now?"

> "Today, by default: your file is deleted within thirty days and you get written
> certification, exactly as the NDA promises. If you want it gone sooner, say the word and
> I will certify deletion this week. If we proceed to the pilot, the data stays only as
> long as the engagement does, same deletion terms at the end. And nothing of yours feeds
> any other customer's analysis: benchmarking across customers happens only if you
> explicitly opt in, in writing, and it is off by default."

Why this works: it is the literal truth, it cites the contract they signed, and offering
faster-than-promised deletion converts a fear question into a trust proof.

## After the call (same day)

- Write the decision-log entry: what they accepted, rejected, deferred, and the dated next
  step.
- Send the recap email: headline number, top-5 table, the one-sentence offer, the agreed
  date. Five lines, no attachments except the readout PDF.
